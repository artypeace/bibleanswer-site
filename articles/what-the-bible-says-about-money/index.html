Money and generosity

What does the Bible say about money?

Begin by distinguishing money from the place it can take in a person's life. These passages ask where we put our hope and how we treat other people.

Does the Bible say that money itself is evil?

In 1 Timothy 6:6–10, the warning concerns the desire to become rich and the love of money. The paragraph also speaks about contentment and ordinary needs. Reading the whole passage helps keep those distinctions together instead of reducing it to a slogan about money itself.

The passage asks us to examine desire and its consequences. It does not say that a person's income tells us how faithful they are. Having little and having much can each bring real responsibilities; neither lets us know another person's heart.

What responsibility does the passage give to people with wealth?

Later in the chapter, 1 Timothy 6:17–19 addresses people who are rich. It warns against arrogance and relying on uncertain wealth, and directs them toward good works, generosity, and willingness to share. Read that paragraph alongside the earlier warning.

The focus is more than accumulating or rejecting possessions. It concerns trust and the use of what is available. Generosity appears as a way of acting toward others, rather than a transaction that guarantees a larger financial return.

How can I reflect before an ordinary purchase?

Imagine you want to buy something mainly because another person has it. You might still choose to buy it, but first name the reason honestly. Is it useful, an expression of care, a wish for approval, or an attempt to make an uneasy feeling disappear? The question is our practical reflection, not an extra command inserted into the passage.

Three questions before the next choice

  • What need or desire am I trying to answer?
  • Am I expecting this purchase to give me worth or certainty it cannot provide?
  • How does this choice sit alongside my responsibilities and willingness to help others?

Use the questions without judging someone else's circumstances. A visible purchase does not tell you a person's whole life. The exercise asks you to examine your own motives and conduct.

Does giving mean that I will become wealthy?

These passages do not promise that a gift will produce a larger sum of money. They call for good works and sharing, while directing hope away from uncertain wealth. Keep the invitation to generosity distinct from a claim about investment returns.
