Getting started

Where to start reading the Bible

Begin with one book, one passage, and a question you can carry into the day. You do not have to understand the whole Bible before you begin.

Choose a first book you can stay with

The Bible contains several kinds of writing: narrative, poetry, letters, and more. Reading from the first page is one possible approach, but it is not the only useful starting point. If you want to become familiar with Jesus, a Gospel offers a clear focus. You can follow its people, conversations, and events instead of moving between unrelated verses.

The Gospel of John is one possible beginning. Near its close, the writer explains why these particular signs have been recorded. That gives you a question to keep beside the whole book: what is this account showing about Jesus, and how does it invite its reader to respond?

This is a suggested starting point, not a required order. If a church or trusted reader has given you another plan, you can use the same reading questions with it. Choose a pace that lets you notice what the text actually says.

Try John 1:1–5 as a first reading

Read the opening slowly. Its language introduces the Word, God, creation, life, and light. Some of those ideas will become clearer as you continue. Write down a question rather than forcing an immediate explanation for every phrase.

A verse number helps you find a sentence again; it is not a signal that the sentence stands alone. After reading these five verses, continue through the rest of John 1. Notice how the opening connects with the people and events that follow. You can return to a difficult phrase after you have heard more of the chapter.

Use four questions before applying the passage

  1. Observe: which words, people, actions, or contrasts are actually on the page?
  2. Locate: who is speaking, and what comes immediately before and after?
  3. Ask: what is clear, and which question needs more reading or help?
  4. Respond: what small response follows from this passage, rather than from an idea you brought to it?

Keep observation and reflection separate in your notes. For example, the text uses the image of light. A personal reflection might concern where you need guidance today. The reflection can be meaningful without becoming a claim that the verse predicts the outcome of a particular choice.

Build a small plan you can return to

For a simple first week, read John 1 through John 7 in order, one chapter per reading. If a chapter is too much for one sitting, pause at a paragraph and resume there. These seven readings are an editorial suggestion, not a named plan promised inside the app.

After each reading, write one observation and one question. At the end of the week, look back before adding a new book. Which question has the following chapter helped you answer? Which one would you like to discuss with someone? Missing a day does not require starting the whole sequence again.

For your next reading

  • Open John 1 and read the first paragraph twice.
  • Name one thing the passage says about the Word.
  • Read on, and keep one unanswered question beside you.

Use a guide when the text feels unfamiliar

A book overview can help you find your bearings. A map can help with a place name; a glossary can help with an unfamiliar word. Use these tools to return to the passage with a clearer question. They work best alongside the text you are reading.

Bible Answer brings the Bible reader, The Guide, and reading plans together. When you use an interpretation or a devotional reflection, compare it with the whole passage. The quotation, its context, and your own response are three different things worth keeping clear.
